The School Musical

I never planned to audition for the school musical. Drama kids were a different species—confident, theatrical, able to sing in front of others without their voices cracking. I was none of those things.

But Ms. Brennan, my English teacher, had other ideas. "You have a natural presence when you read aloud," she told me after class. "You should try out for West Side Story."

I laughed. "I can't sing."

"There are non-singing roles," she persisted. "Just try. What's the worst that could happen?"

Somehow, I found myself at auditions, mumbling through a monologue while the drama teacher, Mr. Wright, watched with an unreadable expression. Two days later, the cast list went up. Against all odds, I'd been cast as Doc, the drugstore owner—a small but pivotal role with no singing required.

What followed were the most exhausting, exhilarating two months of my high school career. Rehearsals ran until 6 PM every weekday, with additional weekend practices as opening night approached. I memorized lines, learned stage blocking, and watched in amazement as the musical extravaganza took shape around me.

The dancers perfected their choreography. The tech crew built a two-level set transforming our stage into 1950s New York. The orchestra practiced until the score flowed seamlessly from overture to finale.

On opening night, peering through the curtain at the packed auditorium, my stomach twisted with nerves. What if I forgot my lines? What if I tripped onstage? What if the audience was bored by my performance?

But when the lights came up on my first scene, something unexpected happened. I wasn't me anymore—I was Doc, concerned about the neighborhood kids and their dangerous rivalries. The words flowed naturally, as if I'd been speaking them all my life.

After the final bow, standing among new friends as applause washed over us, I understood what Ms. Brennan had seen in me all along—not a performer, necessarily, but someone willing to step beyond comfort zones into new possibilities.
