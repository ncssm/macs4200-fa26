The History Project

"Oral histories," announced Mr. Garcia, tapping the whiteboard where he'd written those words in his neat block letters. "For your semester project, you'll be recording the stories of people who lived through significant historical events."

A collective groan rippled through the AP U.S. History class. Another big project when we were already drowning in college applications and extracurriculars.

"Before you complain," he continued with a knowing smile, "remember that history isn't just in textbooks. It's in the memories of people all around you—grandparents, neighbors, community members. This is your chance to preserve those stories before they're lost."

Put that way, it didn't sound so bad. I decided to focus on the civil rights movement, specifically school integration in our town. The local historical society pointed me toward Mrs. Eleanor Jenkins, who had been among the first Black students to attend the previously all-white high school in 1964.

Meeting Mrs. Jenkins changed everything. At 74, she still possessed a sharp memory and compelling way of speaking that transported me to those tense days. She described the whispered insults, the teachers who ignored her raised hand, the few white students who defied social pressure to sit with her at lunch.

"It wasn't the dramatic extravaganza you see in movies," she told me. "It was thousands of small moments of courage, day after day after day."

I recorded six hours of interviews, then spent weeks editing them into a 20-minute documentary. I included historical photos from yearbooks and newspapers, maps showing the segregated neighborhoods, and current images of our now-diverse school.

When I presented the project, the room fell completely silent. Even the students who typically scrolled through their phones during presentations watched attentively. Mr. Garcia had tears in his eyes when the screen went dark.

"This," he said simply, "is why we study history."
