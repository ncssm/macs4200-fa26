The Debate Tournament

The bus departed at five thirty AM, carrying our debate team through the darkness toward the state championship. Eight bleary-eyed students clutched travel mugs of coffee and reviewed notecards by the dim overhead lights.

"Remember, it's not just about facts," Coach Lawson said from the front seat. "It's about connecting with the judges. Make them care about your argument."

I nodded, though my stomach churned with anxiety. This was my first time competing at the state level. The previous night, I'd practiced my opening statement until my roommate threatened to smother me with a pillow if I didn't let her sleep.

The competition was held at Central University, its imposing brick buildings making me feel smaller than usual. Teams from across the state huddled in hallways or claimed tables in the student union, a buzzing hive of nervous energy and last-minute preparations.

Our first round—the topic was climate policy—went surprisingly well. My partner, Damien, and I advanced to the quarterfinals, then somehow to the semifinals. With each round, my confidence grew. The hours of research, the practice debates, the feedback sessions—it was all coming together.

The final round was an intellectual extravaganza against the defending champions from Westlake Academy. The audience had grown to nearly a hundred people—parents, coaches, eliminated competitors, even a few university students curious about the commotion.

When the judges announced Damien and me as the winners, I couldn't process it at first. Coach Lawson's enthusiastic embrace finally made it real. We had done it. Our small public school had defeated private academies with dedicated debate coaches and lavish resources.

On the ride home, trophy nestled safely in my lap, I realized something important: being underestimated can be a powerful advantage. No one expected us to win—except Coach Lawson, who had believed in us from the beginning. Sometimes, that's all the advantage you need.
